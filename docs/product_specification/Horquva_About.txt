ABOUT HORQUVA
We believe organizations should be able to see what they depend on.
Before it becomes a surprise.
Organizations are usually described by their formal structures. Departments. Reporting lines. Functions. Systems. Policies. Processes.
That description is necessary. It is also incomplete.
The way an organization actually operates depends on a much larger set of relationships than any of those structures represent. A decision depends on information held by one person, produced by another team, stored in a particular system, and supplied by an external provider. A process appears routine while quietly relying on undocumented knowledge accumulated over years. A customer-facing operation depends on several internal and external systems working in a particular sequence. An AI system embedded in a workflow depends on a model, a configuration, a set of permissions, and a chain of human decisions that nobody has ever drawn on a single page.
None of this is a failure of management. It is what happens when complexity accumulates without a shared representation. Most individual decisions made sense at the time. None of them were designed with the whole picture in mind. The structure emerged.
And emergent structures are the hardest to understand, because there is no canonical source of truth. The truth is spread across hundreds of partial views, and no single person has ever held all of them at once.
The asymmetry worth noticing
We can model a distributed software system with more precision than we can model the organization that built it.
A service goes down at 3am. Within seconds, you have traces, dashboards, dependency graphs, and alerts that help show where the failure propagated.
Now consider a senior engineer leaving. Someone who was the only person who understood how the billing reconciliation actually worked. How long does it take to find out what depended on them?
Weeks. Sometimes months. Sometimes never, until something breaks six months later and nobody can figure out why.
The difference between these two scenarios is not intelligence, effort, or tooling quality. It is that we built the tools for one and not the other.
We can see the software. We cannot see the organization.
Dependencies are the unit that matters
As organizations grow, their complexity does not grow only through the addition of people or systems.
It grows through dependencies.
Every new system creates relationships with existing systems. Every new process introduces new points of reliance. Every new employee acquires knowledge that may become operationally essential. Every external provider creates another point of dependence. Every automation changes the way work moves.
AI accelerates this in a specific way. Organizations are no longer just adding software to operations. They are placing systems capable of interpreting information, making recommendations, taking actions, and participating in decisions inside real workflows. The resulting environment is not simply an organization with AI added to it. It is an organization whose operations increasingly include computational actors that carry their own dependencies, their own failure modes, and their own opacity.
The important question stops being what AI systems do we have and becomes how are intelligent systems participating in the organization, and what does the organization now depend on them for.
Knowledge is infrastructure
A significant portion of organizational knowledge is never designed as a knowledge system. It develops through experience.
Someone learns why a process is performed a particular way. Someone else understands an exception that is documented nowhere. A technical lead remembers a configuration decision made years ago. A manager knows which decisions require context that never appears in a report.
This knowledge can be operationally essential while remaining structurally invisible. When the people carrying it leave, the organization does not lose an employee. It loses part of its operational memory.
Treating organizational knowledge as infrastructure, not as something that happens to reside inside individuals, changes the kind of system you build.
What Horquva is building
Horquva is a platform for seeing the relationships behind critical work.
The first version maps dependencies across people, systems, vendors, and processes. From there, the model extends to knowledge, AI models, and the other components an organization depends on. The goal is a living structure that stays current and shows what depends on what, who owns what, and what a change in one part of the organization means for the rest.
The work develops in layers.
The first is visibility.
An organization can see its own dependency structure in one place, often for the first time.
The second is reasoning.
Not just showing relationships, but understanding them. Which dependencies matter most. Where concentration is dangerous. What would be difficult to replace. What a change would actually affect.
The third is memory.
Once the structure is continuously observed, it becomes possible to understand how it changes over time. What shifted. Which dependencies became more concentrated. Where new exposure appeared. What decisions were made and what followed.
That is where the system stops being something people consult and starts becoming a record of how an organization changes over time.
First principles, not finished theory
Horquva is not presenting a completed theory of organizational intelligence. The underlying problem is genuinely hard, and pretending otherwise would be a disservice to the organizations we want to work with.
An organization is not a database. There is no schema. No foreign key telling you that this process depends on that vendor. No query language that returns every person whose knowledge is a single point of failure.
What exists is the structure itself. Real, consequential, and almost entirely unrepresented.
Our work is centered on building a model of that structure. We are developing the architecture, testing assumptions, studying real operational environments, and refining the model against contact with how organizations actually behave. That means finding where our models fail, not just where they succeed.
What we are building toward
The long-term objective is a system through which an organization can understand its own structure and how that structure evolves.
A model of the organization and the relationships that allow it to function, one that stays current as people join and leave, processes change, systems are replaced, vendors shift, policies evolve, new dependencies appear, and AI systems become embedded in operations. The model cannot stay static because the organization does not.
This is why organizational intelligence, properly understood, is not a mapping exercise. It is an ongoing system.
Organizations have systems for managing work. Systems for managing data. Systems for managing people, security, finance, and operations. What they do not have is a system that answers the question connecting all of them.
How does the organization itself fit together?
That is the question Horquva is being built to answer.
The immediate work is technical and practical. The larger ambition is a system through which complex organizations can understand themselves. Their dependencies. Their knowledge. Their operational structure. How change propagates through them.
